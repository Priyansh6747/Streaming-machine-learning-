    01 — Mapping transformer knowledge onto quant trading¶

    One-line version: your transformer knowledge transfers to quant trading through the habits (learned representations, attention over context, the eval mindset) — but the problem shape is different: financial data is non-stationary, low signal-to-noise, and the ground truth (future returns) is only knowable in hindsight.

    The good news: the fit is real¶

    Finance is a data-stream problem at its core. Prices tick continuously, regimes change, and a model that doesn't adapt dies. The streaming ML skills you've been building are directly market-shaped. And your transformer background gives you a head start on the representation side. This is not a stretch — the two fields genuinely overlap.

    What your transformer knowledge already covers¶

    Transformer muscle Quant trading equivalent Transfers?
    Learned embeddings of tokens Learned feature representations of market state Yes, core skill
    Attention over a context window Attention over recent price/order-flow context Yes, with care
    The train / val / test split ritual Walk-forward / backtest with no leakage Concept, re-tooled
    The eval harness (accuracy, F1) Sharpe, drawdown, hit rate, per-trade stats Concept, re-tooled
    Positional encodings Time features — time-of-day, session, calendar effects Yes
    Sequence modeling (LSTM/attention) Modeling price paths as sequences Yes, but overfit-prone
    The loss as a training signal The P&L / alpha as the real objective Re-framed
    Batching, epochs, i.i.d. assumption Non-stationary, ordered, drifting data No — must unlearn

    Where attention genuinely helps in markets¶

    Attention is not useless in finance — it's just differently useful than in NLP. Three places it earns its keep:

    1. Cross-sectional attention (over assets, not time): which of 500 stocks matter most right now for predicting a target? Cross-sectional attention is a natural, defensible use — the "context" is the market, and which assets matter shifts with regime.
    2. Feature selection over windows: attention over a rolling window of features (not raw prices) to decide which recent context matters — e.g., "the last 3 days of volatility matter more than the last 20."
    3. Regime detection: attention weights as a soft indicator of which regime the model thinks it's in — a bridge to the drift-detection side of the project.

    The trap to avoid: attention over raw prices as a universal sequence model is where overfitting lives. Markets are not language; the "next token" is noise-dominated, and attention's power to memorize long patterns becomes a liability, not an asset.

    The honest differences (what's new)¶

    • Non-stationarity. NLP data is mostly stationary. Markets are non-stationary by construction — the distribution that generated yesterday's prices is not today's. This is the single biggest shift.
    • Low signal-to-noise. A good predictive edge is a tiny edge over noise. Transformers are built to extract strong structure; markets have weak structure. The failure mode is fitting noise.
    • Hindsight ground truth. In NLP, labels are in the data. In trading, the label (future return) is a realized value you can only know after the fact — the delayed-label problem in its purest form.
    • Survivorship and leakage. The dataset itself lies: delisted stocks vanish from history, and look-ahead bias is everywhere. Your transformer instinct (clean split, careful features) is the right instinct here.
    • The eval metric is money, not accuracy. Accuracy doesn't compound. Sharpe, drawdown, and win rate do. The objective function changes shape.

    How to use transformer knowledge as leverage¶

    Concrete moves that work:

    1. Steal the eval mindset, upgrade it to walk-forward. The habit of "never evaluate on training data" becomes "never let future data leak into the past." Walk-forward / expanding-window evaluation is the market version of your hold-out discipline.
    2. Reuse the representation layer. Whatever feature engineering you did (embeddings, normalized columns, time features) maps to the market-input pipeline. The model changes; the input hygiene survives.
    3. Think of drift detection as regime monitoring. You know what a rising validation loss means. Drift detectors (ADWIN, DDM) are the streaming version: watch the error/P&L distribution, react when the regime shifts.
    4. Port the optimizer discipline, not the architecture. Start with linear models and gradient-boosted trees on good features before attention. The edge in quant is usually in the features and the risk model, not the architecture.

    The bridge summary¶

    Transformer world Quant trading
    Data Fixed dataset, reusable Ticking, non-stationary, non-reusable
    Samples i.i.d., shuffleable Ordered, drifting, regime-dependent
    Ground truth In the data Realized later (delayed labels)
    Signal Strong structure Weak structure, noise-dominated
    Eval Accuracy / loss Sharpe, drawdown, P&L
    The research problem Scale, generalization Drift, adaptation, edge decay, risk

    What transfers: the eval mindset, representation learning, attention as a concept, time-feature discipline, the toolkit. What doesn't: the i.i.d. assumptions, the "one model fits all" instinct, training to accuracy. What's new (and where the project is): non-stationarity, delayed labels, edge decay, regime detection, risk-aware evaluation.

    Open questions for us¶

    • Is cross-sectional attention (over assets) a defensible use of transformers in a market model, or does it overfit as badly as temporal attention?
    • Can drift detection double as regime detection — giving a trading signal ("the regime changed, adapt the model") rather than just a warning?
    • How does the delayed-label problem (see notes/02-concept-drift.md, research/02-delayed-labels.md) bite quant models specifically — and can the project's delayed-label research be a market research project?
    • What does a minimal honest backtest look like — one that can't be accused of look-ahead bias — for a streaming market model?
