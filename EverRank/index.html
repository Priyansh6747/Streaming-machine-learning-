    EverRank — per-user personalization as a drop-in reranking layer¶

    Product doc — EverLearn's flagship application: a personalization layer that sits between any recommendation system's ranking and its serving.

    One line: EverRank is a drop-in reranking layer. Your existing recsys keeps generating candidates; EverRank reorders them with one tiny streaming model per user — and per session — so the feed reacts to you, not your demographic.

    (Working name — rename in one edit if you want a different one.)

    1. TL;DR¶
    What A reranking layer on top of any existing recommendation system
    Who E-commerce, feeds, streaming, ads — anyone with a ranked list and a user
    The gap Personalization stops at the global model: one model serves everyone, retrained monthly
    The wedge Zero-risk adoption — the layer is a pass-through at worst, so a customer can try it without betting their pipeline
    The engine Per-user + per-session streaming adaptors (KB-scale), learned one interaction at a time, IPS-corrected
    The metric Per-user lift in CTR/conversion, and drift recovery time — how many interactions until the feed notices your taste changed
    The moat The measured self-healing mechanism (Exp-01/02) applied at the level of a single user
    The build order Shadow rerank on one feed → live A/B → session layer → platform

    2. The idea¶

    Modern recommendation systems are already staged: retrieval → ranking → reranking. Retrieval finds a few hundred candidates, ranking scores them with the global model, reranking does a final polish before serving.

    EverRank inserts itself at that last seam — the one every real system already has. The customer's retrieval and ranking keep working untouched; EverRank takes their top-K and reorders it:

    existing system:  retrieval ──► global ranking ──► [EverRank] ──► feed
                    (theirs)      (theirs)          (ours)

    The layer shape is not a compromise — it is the product. Three properties fall out of it, each of which is a hard-won answer to a classic objection:

    • Zero-risk adoption. At worst the layer reproduces the incumbent's ranking, so the customer's downside is "exactly what I have today." No data migration, no rip-and-replace, no trust leap. Integration is one API call between their ranking and their serving.
    • The echo chamber is solved upstream. The candidate list still comes from the global system — trending items, new inventory, collaborative "people like you" — because EverRank can only reorder what it is given. Explore and exploit map cleanly onto the two layers: upstream explores, EverRank exploits. The feed cannot become a filter bubble because the layer cannot narrow the candidate pool.
    • Cold start degrades gracefully. A new user has no data, the adaptor sits at its prior, and the layer reproduces the global ranking. EverRank only diverges from the incumbent when the user gives it evidence — a provable no-harm property, and the sentence that closes a sale.

    3. The problem — personalization stopped at the last mile¶

    Every mainstream recsys is one global model plus per-user state: your embedding, your last-fifty-items history, your bandit posteriors. The state is personal; the function mapping state → score is shared by everyone. The consequences:

    • You get the feed of "people like you," never yours. The global model learns the average of its users. Quirks, niche tastes, and the one weird thing you love are noise to it.
    • One user's taste drift is invisible. When your life changes — new city, new baby, new hobby — that shift is a drop in the aggregate. A monthly retrain cannot see it, and the feed quietly rots for weeks.
    • The retrain cycle is always behind. Between retrains the model is stale by construction, and "retrain more often" only shrinks the window — it keeps the machinery and the delay.

    The customer's feed therefore reacts to the segment, not the person. EverRank's answer is the obvious one: stop learning one model for everyone and start learning one tiny model per person, online, all the time.

    4. The architecture — global, user, session¶

    Three levels, each with a different learning clock:

    global model          (not ours)   items, trends, cold start — retrained monthly
  └─ per-user adaptor (ours)       slow taste — weeks — KB-scale, one step per interaction
       └─ per-session adaptor (ours)  current intent — seconds — ephemeral

    Per-user adaptor. A linear probe on the incumbent's features or embeddings — a vector of a few thousand numbers, a few KB. Each interaction is one gradient step. With 10M users × ~5 KB the whole fleet is tens of GB: object storage plus an LRU hot cache. Small capacity is not a compromise — a user hands you hundreds of samples, and a model bigger than this would have nothing to learn from. The tininess is forced by the data, and it is the right answer.

    Per-session adaptor. The strongest predictor of your next action is your last five. Within a session the feedback is dense and fast (clicks in seconds, not purchases in weeks), so a per-session layer — conditioned on context, reset when the session ends — is where the "current intent" lives. This is the level where engagement platforms actually win, and it is pure streaming: you cannot retrain a batch model mid-session.

    On "every mood." Users are multi-intent (browsing for self vs. gifts, work vs. leisure, 1am doomscroll vs. morning utility), but detecting mood is the wrong frame — it fragments an already-sparse signal. The right version is conditioning on observable context: time of day, device, current category, query terms, session length. The model learns "this user at 1am on a phone" as an implicit intent state, no mood labels required.

    The loop, with the honesty built in:

    serve reorder ──► observe (click: seconds · purchase: days · churn: weeks)
                        │
                        └─► learn_one(x, y), IPS-weighted

    The delayed, partial labels here are the research's Delay Tax, per user — and the IPS weighting is non-negotiable: a reranker only sees clicks on what it chose to show, so its own policy biases its labels. An uncorrected online learner amplifies its own mistakes; this is how reranking layers silently fail in production. EverRank learns counterfactually (inverse propensity scoring, maintained online) from day one.

    5. Why the research is the engine¶

    This product is the project's findings applied at the level of a single user:

    Research result EverRank feature
    E01 — streaming self-heals, frozen never does (frozen 0/11; real streaming recovery 8/11; ≈6.9× less corrected damage_ref) The per-user adaptor recovers within a bounded number of interactions when the user's taste changes; the incumbent's monthly retrain literally cannot see one user's drift in the aggregate
    damage_ref / recovery rate The product's headline metric: interactions × skill lost for this user, while their feed was stale
    The Delay Tax Clicks land in seconds, purchases in days, churn in weeks — the adaptor learns on yesterday's truth, and the honest promise is "bounded staleness," not zero lag
    Selective/biased labels literature The IPS-corrected learner — feedback is conditioned on what EverRank itself chose to show
    Prequential harness The shadow-rerank engine: replay the customer's own interactions, prove per-user lift offline before a live A/B

    The Exp-02 story, told as a product: the frozen model is the customer's feed today; the streaming model is EverRank; the drift is a user whose life changed. One heals, the other never notices.

    6. The honest gotchas¶

    Problem Severity Status & mitigation
    Position bias / feedback loop — the reranker shapes its own labels High IPS online from day one; treated as load-bearing, not an add-on
    Lift concentrates on active users — the long tail stays on the global model Medium By design: active users drive the revenue. The pitch says it plainly
    Per-user evaluation & governance — global NDCG hides that 3% of users got worse Medium Per-user metrics, per-user rollback, versioned snapshots — EverLearn's governance, scaled to millions of models
    Per-mood fragmentation — one model per intent state shatters the data Medium Context conditioning, not mood detection; one adaptor per user, context as features
    Depth ceiling — with ~100 samples per user the adaptor learns coarse taste, not semantics Low The global tower carries the semantics; EverRank is the last mile

    7. Positioning¶

    Reranks your list Per-user model Learns online Notices per-user taste drift
    The incumbent (your customer's system) — ✖ (per-user state, one model) ✖ ✖
    Algolia / Constructor / Recombee / Amazon Personalize ✔ ✖ (one global LTR model) ✖ (periodic retrain) ✖
    TikTok's engagement engine ✔ (in-house) ✖ (global, ultra-fast retrain) partial partial
    EverRank ✔ ✔ ✔ every interaction ✔ headline feature

    One-liner for the sales deck: their reranker is one model for all your users, retrained monthly; ours is one model per user, updated every interaction — and it notices when that user changed.

    And the ecosystem note: EverLearn is the engine and the governance, EverRank is the flagship application, DriftDeck can watch the global model upstream (and, one day, the fleet of per-user adaptors). Three docs, one story: detect the decay, price it, delete it — per user.

    8. The killer demo¶

    Two scenes, both runnable on the customer's own logged data:

    Scene 1 — the drift. Find a user whose behavior changed on a real date (moved city, new baby, new category of purchases). Replay: the incumbent's monthly-retrained feed stays stale for weeks after that date; EverRank's adaptor pivots within a bounded number of interactions. Show the damage area as engagement lost, priced in churn risk. This is Exp-02, rerun on one real user.

    Scene 2 — the session. Replay a single session. Watch the feed pivot on the last five actions: the user was shopping for themselves, then searched a gift category, and the incumbent's session-blind ranking keeps serving the old intent for the rest of the evening while EverRank's session layer has already turned. No drift needed — just speed. This is the demo that makes product teams feel the gap.

    9. The build order¶

    Phase Build Exit criterion De-risks
    A — Shadow rerank Offline replay of a real feed: global ranking vs EverRank's per-user adaptor, IPS-corrected, on held-out interactions Per-user lift vs incumbent on real data — without a single live request The mechanism survives real data; the demo exists
    B — Live A/B The layer in production, same candidates, two orderings; per-user rollback and snapshots Statistically honest lift in CTR/conversion; rollback proven in anger Governance under fire; first dollar value attached
    C — Session layer Per-session adaptor on top, context conditioning Measurable mid-session pivot lift The second, defensible moat (speed, not just drift)
    D — Platform Multi-customer, on-prem tier, integrations with the major recsys stacks Paying customers Business

    The research roadmap slots straight in: Exp-05 (delayed labels) is Phase A's core learning risk, and the selective-labels literature already in the review is Phase A's IPS design. The experiments are the product's first milestones.

    10. Business model (brief)¶

    • Pricing per request or per lift — the layer's value scales with traffic and with the engagement it recovers, not with headcount.
    • The ROI number is built in — per-user lift × traffic = recovered engagement, reported by the product itself, and that figure is the subscription justification.
    • Land-and-expand — the zero-risk layer is the wedge into an account that already runs a recsys; EverLearn's governance platform and DriftDeck's monitoring are the expansion.

    11. One-screen recap¶

    Product. EverRank — a drop-in reranking layer. The customer's recsys generates the candidates; EverRank reorders them with one tiny streaming model per user, and per session.

    Why it exists. Personalization stops at the global model: one model for everyone, retrained monthly, so you get the feed of "people like you" — and it can't notice when your taste changed.

    The wedge. Zero-risk adoption: worst case the layer is a pass-through, integration is one API call, and with no user data it provably reproduces the incumbent's ranking.

    The architecture. Global (theirs, explores) → per-user adaptor (slow taste, KB-scale, learns online) → per-session adaptor (current intent, seconds). Echo chamber solved by construction: the layer can only reorder, never narrow.

    The metric. Per-user lift in CTR/conversion, plus drift recovery time — how many interactions until the feed notices your life changed.

    The honest parts. IPS-corrected learning is mandatory (the reranker shapes its own labels); lift concentrates on active users; per-user governance at millions-of-models scale is the real engineering.

    The evidence. Exp-01/02's self-healing mechanism — streaming recovers in bounded time, frozen never does — applied at the level of one user, whose taste drift is exactly the concept drift the project measures.

    The build order. Shadow rerank on one feed → live A/B → session layer → platform.
