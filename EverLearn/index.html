    EverLearn — models that never stop learning¶

    Product doc — turning the streaming-learning research into a product.

    One line: EverLearn is a model-serving layer where the model is never stale, because it learns from every prediction it makes — one sample at a time, in bounded memory, with no retraining pipeline.

    (Working name — rename in one edit if you want a different one.)

    1. TL;DR¶
    What A self-learning serving layer: predict now, learn on the next label, forever
    Who Teams running high-velocity ML — fraud, ads, personalisation, recommendations, moderation
    The gap Every production model is frozen between retrains. The staleness window is where money leaks
    The wedge Real-time ML made the features real-time; the model never got there. EverLearn does
    The engine River / DeepRiver streaming learners — O(1) time and bounded memory per sample
    The metric Staleness saved = damage area avoided × cost-per-error, in dollars
    The moat A measured mechanism (Exp-01/02) plus governance that makes self-modifying models safe to deploy
    The build order Shadow mode on fraud → live on one stream → governance → platform

    2. The idea¶

    Almost every ML product runs the same ritual: train a model, freeze it, deploy it, watch it silently rot until someone reruns the pipeline. The model is a finished object that is periodically rebuilt.

    Streaming learning inverts that. The model is never finished — it is permanently mid-training. Every sample that flows through it follows the same three steps: predict, score, learn, discard. The label arrives (now, or later), the model takes one bounded-memory update, and the world's most recent truth is already inside it. There is no retrain event because there is nothing to retrain: the model is always as current as the last sample it saw.

    The product is that inversion, packaged. EverLearn wraps any model and makes it self-updating — the customer stops maintaining a retraining pipeline and instead owns a model that cannot go stale by construction.

    This is deliberately not a drift-detection product. DriftDeck answers "is my model wrong?"; EverLearn answers "make it stop being wrong — automatically." Detection and adaptation are the two halves of the research; DriftDeck is the watch, EverLearn is the act.

    3. The problem — the staleness window¶

    Between one retrain and the next, the world moves and the model doesn't. The model is silently wrong for the whole window, and two costs compound inside it:

    Cost 1 — the decay itself. Accuracy leaks continuously from the moment of deployment. A weekly retrain means the model is, on average, half a week stale — and that staleness is invisible to the team that owns it until someone measures it.

    Cost 2 — the retrain machinery. The pipeline that rebuilds the model is itself a product-scale investment: feature stores, training jobs, validation, registry, rollout, rollback. MLOps teams spend a large share of their effort feeding a cycle that is, structurally, always behind the data.

    The research already priced cost 1. On a drifting stream, the frozen model never recovered — its corrected damage_ref was 515.0 ± 97.1 samples·κ across 11 seeds, versus 74.8 ± 61.8 for the streaming model (≈6.9×), and a model that retrains on a schedule can only heal at the tick, staying wrong for up to a full interval by pure luck of phase. The staleness window is not an accident of bad ops; it is the defining property of batch-trained models.

    Why this is a product and not a feature: every "retrain faster" fix reduces the window but keeps it — and keeps the machinery. Only a model that learns from the stream itself eliminates both at once. That is the offer.

    4. The wedge — features are real-time, models are frozen¶

    The real-time ML market already proved the demand for lower staleness. Tecton, Chalk, Fennel and friends made features real-time: sub-second freshness for the inputs to the model. But the model those features feed is still batch-trained — retrained nightly or weekly, deployed through a registry.

    So the industry is currently real-time until the last mile, then frozen. A fraud model consumes a feature computed 100 ms ago, but its weights were decided three weeks ago. That last mile — the model's own knowledge — is the entire staleness that remains, and nobody sells it as a product.

    Camp Examples What they made real-time What EverLearn changes
    Real-time feature platforms Tecton, Chalk, Fennel, Featureform Features (the inputs) Nothing — EverLearn sits downstream of them
    Serving + retrain platforms Seldon, Vertex, SageMaker Deployment, not learning The retrain job disappears
    Online-learning libraries Vowpal Wabbit, River The algorithm, as OSS The operational product around it
    EverLearn — The model itself The model's knowledge updates with every sample

    The differentiation is one sentence: everyone else made the inputs fresh and left the model frozen. EverLearn makes the model fresh.

    5. What the customer actually buys¶

    Not "streaming ML" — nobody buys a mechanism. They buy the end of the staleness window, delivered as three things:

    1. The SDK. Wrap any model: EverLearn(model=your_model) preserves the .predict() contract; the learning happens invisibly when each label lands. Drop-in for sklearn / River / PyTorch serving paths.
    2. The engine. River / DeepRiver learners underneath — O(1) time and bounded memory per sample, so one node learns continuously at line rate instead of running batch jobs.
    3. The governance. The honest reason teams don't ship self-modifying models is fear. EverLearn's controls exist to make that fear manageable: shadow mode (learn silently, measure, deploy nothing), snapshots (the model is a sequence of versions, rollback to any of them), learning dials (per-model learning rate, sample cap, what-not-to-learn rules), and an audit log (what the model learned, when, from what).

    The headline metric the product reports: staleness saved — the damage area the self-learning model avoided versus the customer's scheduled-retrain baseline, times their cost-per-error. The research's damage-area metric becomes the customer's dollar figure.

    6. The evidence — the mechanism is already measured¶

    The product's founding claim — a model that keeps learning recovers; a frozen one never does — is not a pitch, it is Exp-01/02 plus the E01 metric correction:

    Fact Number Source
    Frozen batch model recovered after drift 0 / 11 seeds E01
    Streaming model recovered (real excursion) 8 / 11 seeds; the other 3 never left the band E01
    Damage paid while wrong, corrected damage_ref 74.8 ± 61.8 vs 515.0 ± 97.1 samples·κ — ≈ 6.9× E01
    Damage over the rest of the stream (damage_full) 321.4 vs 3,472.9 samples·κ — ≈ 10.8× E01
    Worst streaming seed vs best frozen seed 0.74×, not 24× E01
    Streaming self-heal on the pilot stream back inside the pre-drift band 834 samples after drift on seed 42; frozen fell 0.75 → 0.59 κ and never returned Exp-01

    The legacy 294× mean and 24× worst-vs-best figures were upper bounds from a truncated integral and are retired. The durable claim is the 0/11 frozen recovery and the corrected ≈6.9× mean damage gap.

    The honest caveat, carried over from the research: this is one synthetic stream, two models, no delayed labels, no real data. It proves the mechanism, not the market. The product's first engineering phase exists to close exactly that gap — on the customer's own stream, in shadow mode, where the proof doubles as the sales demo.

    7. The two killer use cases¶

    Streaming learning wins in two different places, and it is worth keeping them distinct because they sell differently:

    A. Self-healing serving — the drop-in upgrade. Fraud detection, ads, content moderation: the model consumes real-time features (already built), but its weights go stale. EverLearn sits behind the existing serving API; predictions are unchanged; the model simply keeps learning from each confirmed label (chargeback, conversion, verdict) as it arrives. The sale is "your current model, minus the decay between retrains." The killer demo: run the customer's own stream through a frozen model and the self-learning model side by side — the frozen one drifts off and stays off, the EverLearn model dips and heals itself live (this demo is Exp-01, rerun on their data).

    B. Per-entity models — where streaming is the only option. One lightweight model per user, session, device, or seller, updated on every interaction, on-device or server-side. Batch systems cannot build per-user models at all — they fake it with embeddings and global retrains. Streaming learning makes per-entity models trivial: a few hundred bytes of state per entity, one update per event, personalisation that reacts to the user's last ten actions, not last month's global retrain. This use case has no batch competitor, which makes it the cleanest market entry.

    8. How it works mechanically¶

    The loop is the prequential harness from the research, productised:

       request ──► predict_one(x) ──► serve ŷ            (unchanged, fast path)
                                      │
                         (label arrives — now or days later)
                                      │
             join (prediction ↔ label) via the event backbone
                                      │
             learn_one(x, y)          (O(1), bounded memory)
             metric.update(y, ŷ)      (rolling κ, damage area, staleness saved)
             snapshot policy          (version the model when skill moves)

    Every sample is scored before it is learned — that is what makes the reported skill honest — and nothing is stored. The infrastructure mirrors DriftDeck's: an SDK that emits (id, features, ŷ), an event backbone (Kafka at scale, Redis Streams for one node) with a predictions topic and a labels topic, and a join buffer sized by the customer's maximum label delay. The engineering hard part is the same two-stream join; the learning itself is the cheap part.

    9. The hard problems — honest¶

    The idea is strong; these are the things that kill it if ignored:

    Problem Severity Honest status & mitigation
    The Delay Tax on the learner — labels arrive late, so the model learns yesterday's truth, and drift is exactly when it matters most High This is the project's open problem. The product's first experiment is a delayed-label learner; until then EverLearn can only promise bounded staleness, not zero lag
    Trust & governance — teams will not deploy a model that changes itself High Shadow mode first (measure, don't deploy), snapshots + rollback, audit log, per-model learning dials. Governance is the product, not an add-on
    Catastrophic forgetting — bounded-memory continual learning degrades on long streams Medium Hybrid design: streaming core + periodic offline consolidation. Known solutions; needs engineering, not research
    Poisoned labels — a model that learns from everything is exposed to bad labels Medium Learning dials (blocklists, confidence gates, anomaly fences) at the ingestion point
    Regulation — some industries require frozen, validated models Medium Target the ones that don't (fraud, ads, personalisation, moderation); on-prem tier for the rest
    It is not a category — nobody has budget for "streaming ML" High Sell the outcome: "never stale," "no retrain pipeline." The mechanism stays under the hood

    10. Positioning¶

    Detects staleness Removes staleness Model keeps learning Per-entity models
    Retrain pipelines (the status quo) ✖ ✖ (only at the tick) ✖ ✖
    Real-time feature platforms ✖ ✖ ✖ ✖
    Drift monitors (incl. DriftDeck) ✔ ✖ (tells you to retrain) ✖ ✖
    Vowpal Wabbit / River (OSS) — ✔ ✔ ✔
    EverLearn ✔ (built-in, as a by-product) ✔ by construction ✔ ✔ native

    The honest position: EverLearn does not compete with drift monitors — it is the thing they tell you to do, done automatically. The research's own pairing is the strategy: DriftDeck prices the decay, EverLearn deletes it. A customer that buys one is one sentence away from buying the other.

    11. The build order¶

    Ordered so the product de-risks itself and the research's next experiments become the product's first milestones:

    Phase Build Exit criterion De-risks
    A — Shadow SDK + engine + label-join + damage accounting, on one real stream (CreditCard fraud), shadow mode only The self-learning model measurably beats the customer's scheduled-retrain baseline on staleness saved — without being deployed The mechanism survives real data; the killer demo exists
    B — Live Snapshots, rollback, one pilot model learning in production The pilot runs live with an owner who can revert it in one action Governance under fire; first real dollars attached to staleness saved
    C — Platform Multi-model, per-entity models, alerts, integrations (registry, feature stores), auth/tenancy Several models, one customer, zero retrain jobs left for them Product–market fit signal
    D — Distribute Hosted + on-prem; OSS the SDK and engine Paying customers Business

    The research roadmap slots straight in: Exp-05 (label delay) is Phase A's core engineering risk; Exp-03/04 (baseline ladder, real streams) are Phase A's shadow-mode validation written in academic form. No wasted work — the experiments are the product's first build phases.

    12. Business model (brief)¶

    • OSS core — the SDK and the engine. Cheap to give away (the algorithms are the research; River is already OSS) and it builds distribution in the MLOps community.
    • Paid — the operational layer: the label-join infrastructure, snapshots and rollback, the audit log, multi-model management, SSO, support. Hosted or on-prem.
    • Pricing — per model or per event, not per seat: the value scales with the staleness the customer avoids, and the "staleness saved" dollar figure the product reports is the ROI number that justifies the subscription.

    13. One-screen recap¶

    Product. EverLearn — a self-learning serving layer. Models that learn from every prediction, in bounded memory, with no retraining pipeline.

    Why it exists. Every production model is frozen between retrains, silently leaking money through the staleness window, and the retrain machinery that fights it is itself expensive and always behind.

    The wedge. Real-time ML made features real-time; EverLearn makes the model's own knowledge real-time. The last mile nobody sells.

    The offer. Drop-in SDK, River/DeepRiver engine, and governance (shadow mode, snapshots, rollback, audit) that makes self-modifying models safe to deploy.

    The metric. Staleness saved — damage area avoided × cost-per-error, in dollars.

    The evidence. E01: a streaming model self-heals after drift (8/11 seeds with a real excursion; the other 3 never left the band); a frozen model never does (0/11), paying ≈6.9× more corrected damage_ref. The mechanism is measured, not promised.

    The honest gaps. Delayed labels weaken the learner exactly when it matters (the Delay Tax — the project's open problem), and self-modifying models need governance before any team will ship them. Both are Phase A.

    The build order. Shadow mode on fraud → live pilot → platform → distribute — with the research's own experiments as the first milestones.
