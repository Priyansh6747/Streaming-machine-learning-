    Progress log — from a question to a measured answer¶

    What this page is: a plain-language record of what we set out to do, how we are going about it, and what we have actually finished — written so that someone who understands “the world changes under a model” but has no machine-learning background can follow it.

    Last updated: 2026-11-14.

    1. The aim¶

    We want to answer one question honestly:

    When the world changes, does a decision system that keeps learning do meaningfully better than one that was trained once — and what does it cost when the correct answers arrive late?

    That is the whole project. Everything else is there to make the answer fair, repeatable, and hard to attack.

    The final deliverable is not a chatbot. It is a small decision system that learns one example at a time, notices when it has become wrong, adapts, and reports how confident it is. Around it sits a measuring lab that anyone can re-run.

    2. What “drift” means here¶

    A decision system learns from examples. If the world that produced those examples changes, yesterday’s lessons can become today’s mistakes.

    Examples:

    • Fraud patterns change, so a fraud detector trained last year misses this year’s tricks.
    • A spam filter trained on old spam starts letting new spam through.
    • A shop’s customers change, so a recommendation rule trained on last season’s buyers becomes stale.

    That is drift: the relationship between the situation and the right answer has moved.

    A second problem makes it harder: the correct answer often arrives late. A loan’s default is known months later; a fraud confirmation may take weeks; a customer’s real preference shows up only after several interactions. The system has to keep working while it is still waiting for the truth.

    3. What we measure, in plain words¶

    We do not want a single “average accuracy” number, because averages hide the moment that matters. A system can look fine on average and still be badly wrong for a long stretch after a change.

    So we measure four things:

    What we measure Plain meaning
    Recovery rate After a real change, how often does the system get back to its old standard?
    Recovery time When it does recover, how many new examples does it need?
    Damage score How wrong was it, and for how long? Think of the area of the “wrongness × time” region.
    Cost How much computing time and memory did that recovery take?

    The key comparison is always:

    • a frozen system — trained once, then never updated; and
    • a learning system — updates itself after every example.

    Both see exactly the same examples in exactly the same order. The only difference is whether the system is allowed to keep learning.

    4. How we are proceeding¶

    The work is a sequence of gates, not a pile of experiments. Each step is only “done” when the step before it is trustworthy.

    1. Make the ruler honest. Build a repeatable lab and fix the way damage is measured, before trusting any comparison.
    2. Test the early-warning signals. Different kinds of change need different alarms. Find out which alarm is reliable, how fast it rings, and how often it cries wolf.
    3. Compare against the honest competition. Not just a frozen system. Also a system that is retrained on a calendar, and a system that is allowed to peek at when the change happened.
    4. Build the learning decision system. A compact model that keeps learning, remembers earlier situations, and stays confident and calibrated.
    5. Measure the cost of late answers. Delay the correct answers on purpose and see what breaks first: the warning, the learning, or the score.
    6. Test on real-world records. Move beyond computer-generated streams.
    7. Write the final report. Only from numbers that can be re-run.

    This order matters. A fair ruler before a comparison; a fair comparison before a new model; a measured model before a claim.

    What changed because of E01¶

    • The old 294× damage gap was a side effect of a ruler that cut off the learning system’s damage. It is retired.
    • The old 24× “worst run vs best run” claim did not survive. On the clean panel the worst learning-system run still pays 0.74× the damage of the best frozen run, so the comparison is categorical the other way.
    • The old “11/11 recovered” counted “never left its normal band” as a recovery. The corrected count is 8/11 real recoveries.
    • The old 835-example recovery was one lucky run. It was never the result; the pattern across runs is the result.

    What survived¶

    • The frozen system recovered on 0 of 11 runs. This is the strongest and most stable finding.
    • The learning system’s damage was lower on 11 of 11 runs.
    • The mean corrected gap is ≈6.9× in the fixed window and ≈10.8× over the rest of the stream.
    • The learning system still wins the overall averages: 88.3% vs 85.4% correct, with a better agreement score that corrects for chance.

    The no-drift control (E02, first pass)¶

    We also ran the opposite test: no change at all. Two stationary streams, five repeats each, every model and every early-warning signal. Nothing should ring. This is the “cry wolf” test.

    Signal Raw mistake signal Smoothed mistake signal What it means
    PageHinkley 0.055 per 1,000 0.050 per 1,000 Stays calm; meets the strict target.
    ADWIN 0.012 per 1,000 0.548 per 1,000 Calm on the raw signal; overreacts when smoothed.
    KSWIN 0.002 per 1,000 7.667 per 1,000 Calm on the raw signal; very noisy when smoothed.

    Values are the worst host (static HT or adaptive HAT) in the E02 artifact; the acceptance block uses the same worst-host rule.

    Plain reading: smoothing the mistake signal is not free. It makes two of the three alarms fire on a world that has not changed. PageHinkley is the robust default in this setting. This is a useful result for the detector map: the “smoothed” version must be treated as a diagnostic, not as the default alarm.

    The models themselves stayed stable on both stationary streams, which is the sanity check the control was built for.

    The detector map (E04, first pass)¶

    We then ran the same four kinds of change — sudden, gradual, incremental and recurring — across four different computer-generated worlds, five repeats each, with two different base learners and eight early-warning signals.

    After fixing a scoring bug (a signal that never rang was being left out of the average, which made it look perfect), one alarm came out ahead on all four kinds of change: HDDM-A, using the raw mistake signal.

    Kind of change Best signal Balanced score Changes caught False alarms per 1,000
    Sudden HDDM-A, raw 0.68 75% 0.008
    Gradual HDDM-A, raw 0.84 95% 0.008
    Incremental HDDM-A, raw 0.88 90% 0.003
    Recurring HDDM-A, raw 0.50 73% 0.031

    Plain reading:

    • One alarm — HDDM-A on the raw signal — is a sensible default across all four kinds of change.
    • Recurring change is the hardest (balanced score 0.50); incremental change is the easiest (0.88).
    • Smoothing the mistake signal does not help: it raises false alarms without buying a better ranking. The raw signal stays the default; the smoothed one is a diagnostic.
    • The ranking of alarms is mostly the same whether the base learner is static or adaptive; KSWIN is the exception.
    • The scoring bug mattered: before the fix, a signal that almost never rang looked perfect. After the fix, it did not. This is exactly the kind of error the lab is meant to catch.

    Full technical write-up: E04 — Detector map.

    The real-stream detector check (E17)¶

    We re-ran the same eight alarms on real electricity-market records (Elec2, 45,312 rows), shuffled, with a known label-swap change injected halfway through. Five repeats, the same two hosts.

    Kind of change HDDM-A, static host ADWIN, static host HDDM-A, adaptive host ADWIN, adaptive host
    Sudden 0.93 0.45 0.40 1.00
    Gradual 0.96 0.50 1.00 1.00
    Incremental 0.96 0.84 1.00 0.97
    Recurring 0.79 0.51 0.59 0.77

    Plain reading: on the static learner, HDDM-A keeps its four-shape win. On the adaptive learner, ADWIN wins sudden and recurring change. The adaptive learner can repair the mistake rate quickly enough that HDDM-A's one-sided test misses the change; ADWIN compares windows two-sidedly and still rings. The “HDDM-A is the default” claim is therefore true for the static host, not universally. Five repeats and one real dataset: a warning, not a final ranking. Full write-up: E17 — Real-stream detector map.

    The real-stream model check (E14)¶

    We also replayed the model comparison on real features and labels: Elec2 (45,312 rows) and Phishing (1,250 rows), with a known change injected halfway through. Five repeats, five models.

    Model Agreement (κ) Damage paid (damage_ref) Recovered?
    ARF 0.724 198.0 20% of runs
    HAT 0.583 245.7 15%
    DriftFormer 0.524 284.9 0%
    Frozen RF 0.395 297.4 0%

    Plain reading: ARF leads on both real datasets; the deep model does not beat it. That is expected and useful: for DF-H the deep model is a sensor, not the main classifier. The slice is provisional — two datasets, five repeats — so it is a host check, not a field-wide ranking. The acceptance rules pass provisionally (pooled damage ratio 0.666×, ARF lower in 18/20 cells, frozen recovered 0/20). Full write-up: E14 — real-stream model check.

    The scheduled-retrain ladder (E03)¶

    We ran the full 11-repeat ladder on two computer-generated streams: a frozen model, three retrain clocks (1k, 5k, 20k), static and adaptive trees, ARF, Leveraging Bagging, ADWIN Bagging, and the timing oracle. 220 runs.

    Model Damage paid (damage_ref) Recovered?
    Leveraging Bagging 455.7 100%
    HAT 489.2 96%
    ARF 575.4 59%
    ADWIN Bagging 614.1 100%
    Hoeffding Tree 700.8 96%
    Scheduled RF, 1k 733.5 100%
    Scheduled RF, 5k 824.1 100%
    Scheduled RF, 20k 849.4 68%
    Frozen RF 871.7 0%
    Oracle refit 864.3 0%

    Plain reading: every learning-from-the-stream model pays less damage than the frozen model and than every retrain clock. The best is Leveraging Bagging; even the fastest retrain clock (1k) pays 1.6× more damage. The scheduled models “recover” by construction at each tick, so recovery rate is not the comparison — damage is. The phase-noise test is inconclusive: the 12k refit buffer is longer than the 1k and 5k clocks, so the lag is buffer-dominated; only the 20k cell is valid and it has one observation. Artifact: experiments/results/e03/summary.json.

    The KDD'26 benchmark fast pilot¶

    We reproduced the paper's Monte Carlo protocol on seven real datasets at both ten and fifty trials, with the six fastest detectors. The 50-trial run confirms the pilot: SEED and STEPD tie for first (average rank 2.12; mean F1 0.300 and 0.303), HDDM-W is third (3.56; 0.163), and HDDM-A is fourth (3.87; 0.155). DDM (4.54) and ADWIN (4.80) follow. HDDM-A is clearly not the best detector on real data.

    The F1 values also reproduce the paper's tables — for example abrupt feature permutation is 0.572/0.526/0.313 for SEED/STEPD/HDDM-A here vs 0.61/0.53/0.36 published. The ABCD/ABCD(X) arm is not re-run: it is the expensive part of the matrix, it overheats the laptop CPU, and the paper's published result is used for that cell instead.

    6. How the project has benefited¶

    What we did How it helped
    Built a reusable experiment lab Every future result uses the same rules, so a new experiment cannot quietly use easier conditions.
    Fixed the damage ruler We removed the biggest known attack on our own result before anyone else used it.
    Added progress logs and saved files after every run A long run no longer has to be repeated from zero; we can see where time goes.
    Wrote the correction into the live documentation There is one place for the current numbers; old pages carry a warning, not a contradiction.
    Kept the old results The change is auditable: anyone can see what we believed, why it was wrong, and what replaced it.

    7. What we are not claiming¶

    Honesty is part of the method. We are not claiming:

    • that the learning system always wins;
    • that 294× is the true size of the effect (it is not);
    • that the result holds on real-world records yet;
    • that the late-answer problem is solved;
    • that this is a chatbot or a large language model.

    We are claiming a smaller, measured, repeatable result: a system that keeps learning recovers from change, a frozen one does not, and the corrected damage gap is around seven times in the fixed window.

    8. What is next¶

    The detector track is now the project's ultimate goal. The question is no longer "which single alarm is best" — it is "can a label-free deep monitor plus a supervised confirmation beat the detector benchmark, and what happens when the labels arrive late?"

    Ultimate goal: build DF-H — a two-stage detector that combines a label-free deep representation monitor with supervised confirmation — and show that it (a) is top-2 on the KDD'26 benchmark with wins on the feature and recurring cells, and (b) keeps the most detection power under delayed, partial and selective labels.

    In order:

    1. Signal gate (E18). ✅ ran — negative: best deep AUC 0.616 vs error 0.598. The timing diagnostic is positive on feature drift, but the classical KS control is competitive.
    2. DF-H v1 (E19). ✅ ran — gate failed: DFH 3rd overall (7.09) behind STEPD 5.22 and SEED 5.44; FAR passes, top-2 and primary feature-cell checks fail.
    3. DF-H v2 (E20). Not run. The pilot-first rule says the full matrix is not justified; a learned gate would need more datasets than the pilot has.
    4. Delay Tax benchmark (E21). ✅ fixed-delay headline measured: at L = 2,000, Stage 1 warns in 70–513 samples vs SEED/STEPD 2,120–2,710 and ADWIN 2,472–3,269; Stage 1 is flat in L. The stochastic/partial/selective/batch matrix and Lemmas 2–3 are scaffolded but not run.
    5. Final report. Only from saved, re-runnable numbers.

    The older breadth-first experiments (E05, E08–E13) are deferred; E15 is replaced by E21. See Roadmap v2 for the full plan and gates.

    Detector-pilot status (2026-11-14). The E18 instrumentation is complete (signal_snapshot(), DeepSignalMonitor, signals.npz, pure-NumPy build_trial_stream, experiments.deep_kdd precompute/report/detect). E18's AUC gate failed, but at 0.1/1k FAR the deep signals detect feature permutation 2–5× earlier than error and detect feature filtering when error is blind. E19's DF-H v1 pilot ranked 3rd, passed FAR, and beat the no-feature control, but failed top-2, the primary feature-cell test and Covtype robustness. The full E20 matrix is not justified. E21 now carries the headline: at L = 2,000, Stage 1 warns in 70–513 samples while the error-only panel takes 2,120–3,269; the label-free warning is not floor-bound. E22 then shows the capability against the requested classical panel: DF-H is 3rd (7.34), ahead of HDDM-W (7.47), ADWIN (9.94), HDDM-A (11.34) and PageHinkley (12.53); only SEED/STEPD lead. The detector remains a supporting warning/confirmation result, with feature permutation as its clear strong cell.

    9. Plain-word glossary¶

    Word What it means here
    Drift The world changes, so yesterday’s lessons become wrong.
    Learner / decision system A rule that is learned from examples and used to make a decision.
    Frozen Trained once, then never updated.
    Learning / streaming Updates itself after every new example.
    Correct answer (label) The truth for one example; often arrives late.
    Early-warning signal (detector) A smoke alarm for change: it rings when the stream starts to look different.
    Damage score How wrong the system was, multiplied by how long it stayed wrong.
    Repeat (seed) One random re-run of the same experiment, to separate a real effect from luck.
    Comparison that peeks A baseline that is allowed to know when the change happened; an upper bound, not a fair opponent.
    Calibration When the system says “70% sure,” it is right about 70% of the time.
