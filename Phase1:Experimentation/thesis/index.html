    Thesis — How streaming models beat batch models, and on what¶

    Purpose: The argument behind the teacher's plan — "take some models that are not streaming and beat them on real metrics." This page states the idea, the established facts it rests on (with sources), the expected course of an experiment, and the results we expect to see. Everything marked illustrative is a pre-registered expectation, to be replaced by measured numbers from the Ch1 run.

    Status: Thesis expanded (idea → facts → expected events → expected results). Derivations and estimator definitions to follow in calculation.md.

    Formatting note: math is written in Unicode (τ, κ₀, Δκ, ≈, ≥) so it renders identically in Obsidian, GitHub, and the mkdocs site — no LaTeX pipeline required.

    1. The idea¶

    On a drifting, unbounded, label-scarce stream, a streaming model with a drift detector returns to pre-drift performance within a bounded number of samples of a concept change, and a batch model does not — because batch models update on a clock, while streaming models update on evidence.

    Three scope clauses make the claim precise rather than grandiose:

    1. "Beat" is per-metric, never global. The claim is about the temporal reactivity family — recovery time, detection delay, performance between retrains, memory, label efficiency. It is explicitly not about raw mean accuracy over the whole stream, where a properly tuned, frequently retrained batch model can win (Fact 7 below).
    2. The comparison is fair. Batch baselines get a warm-up window, get retrained, and see the same data in the same order, evaluated under the same prequential protocol. The asymmetry is structural: a batch model's update schedule does not depend on what the data is doing.
    3. The claim is falsifiable. Section 6 lists the observable outcomes that would sink it. If streaming's recovery time is not bounded and smaller than a sane retrain interval, the thesis is wrong.

    2. Setting and definitions¶

    Let a stream arrive one sample at a time, and let a concept drift occur at time τ, after which the error rate of every model trained on the old concept rises by Δe. Define:

    • d — detection delay: samples from τ until the drift detector alarms.
    • a — adaptation time: samples from the alarm until the model's rolling performance returns to its pre-drift level.
    • r_next — the next scheduled retrain of a clock-driven batch model (possibly ∞, for a model trained once and never updated).
    • k — the retrain interval of a scheduled batch model.
    • w — the window of the rolling metric (e.g. w = 1000 samples).
    • W — the tolerance window: an alarm within W samples of a true drift counts as a detection with delay; an alarm outside any window counts as a false positive. W is chosen explicitly and justified, per notes/04-evaluation.md.

    The recovery time of a model is the number of samples from τ until its rolling performance is back to its pre-drift level.

    3. The mechanism¶

    All models are equally good before τ; the difference is what happens after.

    • Frozen batch (train once, deploy, never update): error rises at τ and stays elevated until an external decision to retrain. Recovery time is undefined — the model has no mechanism that reacts to data.
    • Scheduled batch (refit every k samples, no detector): error rises at τ, but the model only watches its clock. If the last retrain was at τ − 1, it stays wrong for almost k more samples; on average it is wrong k/2 samples too long. Recovery time is r_next − τ — luck of phase.
    • Streaming + drift detector: the detector watches the error stream, alarms at τ + d, adaptation starts immediately, and rolling performance is back to baseline at τ + d + a. Recovery time is d + a — bounded and data-driven.

    The damage a drift causes is the area under the excess-error curve:

    Streaming damage       ≈ (d + a) × Δe
Scheduled-batch damage ≈ (r_next − τ) × Δe
Frozen-batch damage    = unbounded (never recovers on its own)

    Streaming wins whenever d + a < r_next − τ — the generic case, since d + a is typically tens to low hundreds of samples while r_next − τ spans the whole retrain interval. That factor, (r_next − τ) / (d + a), is the quantitative content of the project.

    4. Facts it is based on (with sources)¶

    # Fact Evidence Source (verified in the vault)
    F1 Test-then-train prequential evaluation is the accepted streaming protocol; every sample scores before it trains. Batch train/test splits leak future information on temporal data; each sample is used for testing, then training. Gama, Sebastião & Rodrigues (2013), Machine Learning 90:317–346 — notes/04-evaluation.md
    F2 Drift is detectable online with bounded delay. ADWIN adaptively sizes a window and alarms on significant mean change; DDM tracks error-rate bounds (p ± s: warn at p + 2s, alarm at p + 3s). Bifet & Gavaldà (2009), IDA; Gama et al. (2004), DDM — notes/02-concept-drift.md
    F3 Adaptive streaming learners recover after drift. ARF: adaptive operators + resampling for abrupt, gradual, recurring drift; HAT: ADWIN per tree node. Gomes et al. (2017), Machine Learning 106:1469–1495; Bifet & Gavaldà (2009) — papers/
    F4 Adaptive streaming beats static baselines on real drifting, imbalanced data — with numbers. ROSE: Kappa 63.65 vs next-best 57.85, 24 real imbalanced datasets, 30 ensembles compared. Cano & Krawczyk (2022), Machine Learning 111:2561–2599 — papers/cano-krawczyk-2022-rose.md
    F5 Detector quality is a three-way trade-off (delay / false alarms / misses); no detector wins all three. KDD '26 framework: 14 detectors × 7 real datasets; defines F1-detection score and normalized detection time. Cerqueira et al. (2026), KDD '26, DOI 10.1145/3770855.3819070 — papers/kdd2026-drift-benchmark.md
    F6 Label delay/scarcity starves batch retraining; streaming keeps working. STUDD: best label efficiency among non-baseline methods on 19 streams, MDR ≈ 19%. A 10,000-sample label delay shifts detections by ~10,000 samples and pins prequential accuracy below 50%. Delayed labels raise user exposure ~50%, cut detection up to 75%. Cerqueira et al. (2023), Machine Learning 112:4351–4378; Gomes et al. (2022), ACM Comput. Surveys 55(4):75; Botacin & Gomes (2024), Computers & Security 147 — papers/, research/02-delayed-labels.md
    F7 Counter-evidence: oracle-tuned, frequently retrained batch can beat streaming under delay + rare classes. No-delay benchmark: retrained XGBoost AUC-ROC 92.81 vs ARF 91.02. Delayed-label fraud data: XGBoost AUCPR 23.41 vs ARF 14.23. ARF ~2.2× slower. Amekoe et al. (2024), arXiv:2409.10111 — streaming-vs-batch-metrics.md §3
    F8 Comparing many learners across many streams needs Friedman + Nemenyi; single runs are noise. ≥10 seeds, mean ± SD, critical-difference diagrams; Wilcoxon signed-rank for pairs. Demšar (2006), JMLR 7:1–30 — notes/04-evaluation.md

    What follows from these facts. F2 + F3 give streaming a bounded reaction time d + a. F4 is literature precedent that adaptive models beat non-adaptive ones on exactly the kind of real data we plan to use. F6 gives the label-scarcity win. F7 is the fence: it defines where we must not claim victory, and it is why the Delay Tax (label delay) is the project's open problem rather than a solved one.

    5. Expected events (experiment timeline)¶

    5a. Synthetic streams (ground-truth drift: SEA, Agrawal, Hyperplane, RandomRBF, STAGGER, LED)¶

    A single run (e.g. 100,000 samples, ≥10 seeds) is expected to produce this event sequence; each stage records specific variables:

    # Event What happens What we record
    1 Warm-up First 20–30% of the stream trains all models; no evaluation (excluded)
    2 Steady state Rolling Kappa of adaptive models plateaus near its ceiling baseline level κ₀ (mean over the w samples before drift)
    3 Drift onset at τ The generating concept changes; all models' rolling Kappa starts to dip τ is known by construction
    4 Detector alarm Streaming detector fires at τ + d; batch models are silent d; whether the alarm falls inside tolerance window W
    5 Adaptation Streaming model resets/adapts; rolling Kappa bottoms out, then climbs dip depth; recovery slope
    6 Recovery Rolling Kappa re-enters the κ₀ band and stays recovery time d + a
    7 (batch) next retrain Scheduled batch refits at r_next, recovering only then; frozen never does r_next − τ
    8 Repeat Next drift at the next pre-set position per-drift rows in the summary table

    Expected detector behaviour on abrupt synthetic drift (literature-anchored, to be measured): ADWIN alarms within tens of samples, DDM within low hundreds. On gradual drift both slow down — and that slowdown is itself a result (F5's trade-off), not a bug.

    5b. Real streams (Elec2, Phishing, Covertype, Airlines, CreditCard)¶

    No ground-truth drift points exist, so the event model changes:

    • Drift is inferred from rolling-metric dips and detector alarms; there is no known τ to measure delay against.
    • Comparison targets post-dip behaviour: dip depth, recovery slope, and the fraction of the post-drift window each model spends below its own baseline.
    • Because Elec2's Kappa+ is known to collapse (protocol caveat), the headline on real data is rolling Cohen Kappa + AUC-PR, with Elec2 secondary.

    6. Expected results¶

    Everything below is illustrative and pre-registered — plausible values in the ranges the literature (F1–F7) supports, to be confirmed or refuted by the Ch1 run. No number here is a measured result.

    6a. The picture we expect: rolling Kappa over time¶

    Schematic (illustrative, not to scale) of one synthetic stream with one drift at τ = 25,000, rolling window w = 1,000:

    Figure 1 — the three curves are the whole thesis in one picture: a shallow, short dip that self-heals (streaming, blue); a dip that heals only on the next retrain tick (scheduled batch, orange); a permanent step down (frozen, red). Shaded regions are the damage areas each model pays after the drift.

    6b. Illustrative recovery-time numbers (one drift, synthetic stream)¶

    Baseline rolling Kappa κ₀ ≈ 0.85; dip to ≈ 0.5; means over ≥10 seeds (illustrative, not measured):

    Model d (delay) Recovery time Rolling Kappa at τ + 1000 Damage area (samples × Δκ)
    HAT + ADWIN ~40 ~250 ~0.83 ~50
    Leveraging Bagging + DDM ~100 ~350 ~0.80 ~90
    Scheduled XGBoost (k = 5000) — ~2,400 (phase-dependent) ~0.62 ~900
    Frozen RF (warm-up 20%) — never ~0.45 unbounded

    The decisive column is damage area: streaming pays ~50–100, scheduled batch pays ~an order of magnitude more, frozen pays forever. Note the scheduled model's recovery time is not a property of the model — rerun with different seed phases and it scatters uniformly over [0, k] (pure phase noise, which the ≥10-seed protocol exposes).

    6c. Detector metrics (synthetic, ground-truth)¶

    Detector Expected delay on abrupt drift Expected on gradual drift Trade-off to watch
    ADWIN tens of samples hundreds–thousands fewer false alarms, slower on gradual
    DDM low hundreds weak (assumes improving error) fast on abrupt, false alarms on noise
    Page-Hinkley tens–hundreds moderate threshold-sensitive

    Expectation, not a bug: no detector wins delay, false alarms and misses simultaneously (F5). The Ch1 contribution is reporting the trade-off with real numbers and pricing it in damage area — not claiming a magic detector.

    6d. Resource metrics (measured; structural expectations)¶

    Metric Streaming (expectation) Batch (expectation)
    Memory fixed by model size; flat vs stream length frozen: fixed; scheduled: grows with stored window
    Passes over data 1 ≥1 per refit (scheduled ≈ stream/k passes)
    Throughput bounded per-sample cost (ARF the expensive one) refit bursts, idle between

    Honest losses we expect to report: streaming per-sample cost can exceed batch amortised cost on stationary stretches (ARF ~2.2× per F7); scheduled batch may match streaming rolling accuracy when no drift occurs. The advantage is conditional on change — and we will say so.

    6e. The real-data win card (expected direction per claim)¶

    Dataset Claim we expect to defend Primary metric
    Phishing, Airlines streaming rolling Kappa ≥ frozen RF/XGBoost on post-drift windows rolling Kappa
    Covertype streaming ≥ frozen; scheduled phase noise visible rolling Kappa, damage area
    CreditCard fraud streaming AUC-PR ≥ frozen; tuned batch reported as upper bound AUC-PR (imbalanced; accuracy meaningless)
    Elec2 secondary; rolling Kappa + AUC-PR only (Kappa+ collapse caveat) rolling Kappa
    All datasets Friedman + Nemenyi across datasets; Wilcoxon pairwise; effect sizes critical-difference diagram

    6f. What would falsify the thesis¶

    1. Streaming recovery time comparable to or worse than a sane retrain interval (d + a ≥ r_next − τ on average) on synthetic drifts.
    2. Frozen batch's rolling Kappa not dropping at drift — i.e. no real drift, a dataset problem rather than a model win.
    3. No significant difference after Friedman/Nemenyi/Wilcoxon (α = 0.05, ≥10 seeds).
    4. Streaming's resource cost so high that the reactivity win is not worth it (measured and reported, not hidden).
    5. On the no-drift control stream, streaming loses to scheduled batch on rolling accuracy — expected, but it must be shown and explained, not swept under the rug.

    7. Protocol and statistics¶

    Full experimental protocol (models, datasets, seeds, baselines): see streaming-vs-batch-metrics.md. Statistical design per F8: ≥10 seeds on synthetic; bootstrap windows on real ordered streams; Friedman + Nemenyi across datasets; Wilcoxon signed-rank pairwise; effect sizes. One claim per metric, each with the dataset and number behind it — the win card (§6e) is the deliverable that gets written into Ch1.
