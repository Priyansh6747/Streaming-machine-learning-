    02 — Financial quant fundamentals¶

    One-line version: quant trading is using math and data to make statistically disciplined bets on markets — the edge is small, the data is noisy and non-stationary, and the difference between a good quant and a gambler is the evaluation, risk, and leakage hygiene around the model.

    What "quant" actually means¶

    Quantitative trading = making trading decisions from numerical models and data rather than gut feel. The spectrum:

    • Statistical arbitrage — find and exploit temporary mispricings between related assets (pairs, baskets).
    • Momentum / trend following — the market's tendency to continue in a direction for a while; classic systematic strategy.
    • Mean reversion — the market's tendency to snap back to an average; the statistical opposite of momentum.
    • Market making — profit from the bid/ask spread by providing liquidity.
    • High-frequency (HFT) — microstructure, sub-second edges. Not our lane with streaming ML at sample rates like one bar per minute.
    • Factor investing — long/short portfolios tilted toward known factors (value, momentum, size, volatility).

    For a research project, the accessible lanes are statistical arbitrage, momentum, and mean reversion on moderate-frequency data (minutes to days). HFT is out — it's a latency/microstructure game, not an ML problem.

    The quant pipeline (the mental model to keep)¶

    raw data → features → model → position sizing → execution → P&L
                ↑                                   ↓
                └────── risk & evaluation loop ←─────┘

    Every stage is a discipline:

    1. Raw data — prices, volumes, order flow, fundamentals, alternative data. Hygiene here is everything: adjusted prices (splits/dividends), no look-ahead, survivorship-free universes.
    2. Features — returns, volatility, momentum, ratios, spreads, time-of-day, regime indicators. This is where edges live. Most quant alpha is feature engineering, not architecture.
    3. Model — predicts a target: direction, return magnitude, volatility, or a mispricing signal. Linear models, trees, and (carefully) neural nets. The model is usually the smallest contributor to edge.
    4. Position sizing — how much to bet, given the edge and the uncertainty (Kelly, fractional Kelly, vol targeting). Model confidence alone is not enough.
    5. Execution — actually trading without eating the edge in costs (spread, fees, slippage).
    6. Risk & evaluation — the loop that keeps you honest: Sharpe, drawdown, exposure, leakage checks, out-of-sample discipline.

    Key concepts, in plain terms¶

    • Alpha — the part of a return that isn't explained by the market; the edge your model adds. "Alpha decay" = the edge fading as others find it.
    • Beta — the market's general direction; a long-only portfolio has market beta. Quant strategies try to isolate alpha net of beta.
    • Sharpe ratio — (excess return) / (volatility). The standard scorecard. A Sharpe above 1 is good; above 2 is great. It's the risk-adjusted return.
    • Drawdown — peak-to-trough loss. A strategy with great Sharpe but a 40% drawdown is psychologically and practically hard to run. Drawdown is what actually kills funds.
    • P&L / returns — log returns (continuous compounding) are the natural scale for models; simple returns for portfolio math. Log returns are roughly additive over time, simple returns aren't.
    • Volatility — the standard deviation of returns; the thing that scales with risk. Vol is itself forecastable (GARCH, realized vol) and mean-reverts.
    • Regime — a persistent market state (bull/bear, high/low vol). Regime shifts are concept drift wearing a suit.
    • Bid/ask spread — the cost of trading. If your edge per trade is smaller than the spread, you lose regardless of model quality.
    • Slippage — the difference between your intended and actual fill price. Backtests that ignore slippage are fiction.

    Data types (and their character)¶

    Type Frequency Character Notes
    Ticks ms Noisy, huge HFT lane; microstructure
    OHLCV bars 1m–1d Standard for ML The workhorse: open/high/low/close/volume
    Fundamentals quarterly Sparse, lagged, survivorship-heavy Point-in-time data is the only honest version
    Order book ms Microstructure Depth, imbalance; HFT-ish
    Alternative mixed Novel, sparse Sentiment, satellite, search trends
    Options mixed Derivative structure Implied vol is a rich feature source

    The two data traps that end projects:

    1. Survivorship bias — backtesting only stocks that still exist today. Delisted stocks (which often failed) vanish from the historical dataset, inflating returns. You must include delisted names and mark them dead.
    2. Look-ahead bias — using information not available at the time (future prices, restated fundamentals, same-day close to predict same-day return). The fix is strict time-ordering: features computed before the prediction point, labels after it.

    Backtesting: the honest version¶

    A backtest is a simulation of a strategy on history. It is not proof — it's a sanity check that's easy to fool. The disciplines:

    • Walk-forward evaluation — train on a window, test on the next, roll forward. Never train and test on overlapping periods.
    • Expanding vs rolling window — expanding = use all history so far (more data, adapts slower to regime); rolling = fixed recent window (less data, adapts faster). Streaming ML maps to this directly.
    • Transaction costs — model spread + slippage + fees explicitly. A strategy profitable in zero-cost backtest often dies with realistic costs.
    • Out-of-sample discipline — final evaluation on data never touched during development. This is the quant version of your hold-out test set.
    • Multiple-testing honesty — trying 500 features/strategies and reporting the best one is p-hacking by another name. Report the distribution, not the winner.

    The honest math (what wins and what doesn't)¶

    • The edge is small. A great strategy wins 52–55% of the time, or has a Sharpe of 1–2. Anyone promising much more is selling something.
    • Volatility is the enemy of compounding. Deep drawdowns need larger recoveries: a 50% loss needs a 100% gain to break even. Risk management is not optional — it's the strategy.
    • More complexity = more overfitting risk. Linear models on good features routinely beat deep models on mediocre features, in production.
    • Transaction costs are a tax on frequency. High-frequency strategies die first when costs are realistic. Lower frequency (bars/days) tolerates costs better.
    • The market is adaptive. Whatever edge you find decays as it's exploited — alpha decay is the reason streaming adaptation is required, not optional.

    Tooling landscape¶

    • Python — the ecosystem: pandas (data), numpy (math), scikit-learn / lightgbm / xgboost (models), statsmodels (econometrics).
    • Backtesting — backtrader, vectorbt, zipline, bt.
    • Data — yfinance (free, fine for learning), ccxt (crypto), paid feeds for production (Polygon, EOD, Bloomberg/Refinitiv).
    • Streaming ML — River (our framework) — a natural fit for the online loop that live markets are.
    • Research infra — Jupyter, mlflow (experiment tracking), wandb.

    Open questions for us¶

    • Momentum, mean reversion, or stat-arb as the first strategy family? (Each maps differently onto streaming models.)
    • Which data source: free daily bars (yfinance), free crypto (ccxt), or a synthetic stream (generate regimes ourselves) for a clean research setup?
    • How do we measure a streaming trading strategy — Sharpe computed prequentially, per-bar, with realistic costs?
    • Does the delayed-label angle (labels arrive after the trade decision) make our project uniquely suited to markets, or uniquely unsuited? (I suspect suited — see 03.)
