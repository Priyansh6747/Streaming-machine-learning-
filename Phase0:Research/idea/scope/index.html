    Scope — The Delay Tax¶

    Working title: The Delay Tax: drift detection and adaptation when labels are late, partial, and biased.

    Status: scope locked — pending only the teacher-dependent questions in §12. This document supersedes notes/08-scope.md (kept as the draft it was). It consolidates the research phase (now complete) into a single reference.

    Thesis spine: Streaming ML works in simulation. Does it work in production? Build the system, find where it breaks, fix one thing properly.

    The one thing we fix: production labels arrive late, partially, and selectively — and the field's detectors, metrics, and evaluation protocol quietly assume they arrive instantly, completely, and unbiased.

    1. The problem in one page¶

    Streaming ML rests on a hidden assumption:

    Sample x arrives, and its true label y arrives with it. The model predicts, is immediately told whether it was right, and learns.

    That assumption is false in every real deployment:

    • Fraud: a transaction is approved now; the chargeback lands 30–90 days later.
    • Ads: an impression is served now; the conversion arrives next week.
    • Intrusion: a login is flagged now; the analyst verdict comes later, or never.

    Follow the consequences, because they cascade:

    1. Detection is late. Drift detectors watch the error stream. If labels are late, the error stream is late, so drift is detected only after the labels catch up — meanwhile the model is silently wrong.
    2. Evaluation breaks first. Prequential scoring (predict → score → learn) requires the label in the same tick. Under delay you cannot even measure your own accuracy in real time, so the field's own instrument for success fails in the setting the field claims to serve.
    3. Adaptation compounds the error. While labels are pending, the model keeps learning stale samples — after a drift, it is actively reinforcing the pre-drift concept.
    4. The label process itself misbehaves. Labels arrive in bursts, out of order, or only for flagged cases. That injects autocorrelation and selection bias into the error stream — which detectors misread as drift (false alarms) or miss entirely.

    The research phase (done — §2) confirms this is a real, under-populated gap, not a tutorial topic.

    2. What the research established¶

    Findings from research/01-landscape.md and research/02-delayed-labels.md.

    The gap is real and open. The delayed/partial-label literature is fragmented — terminology alone spans verification latency, delayed labelling, label latency, extreme verification latency, partially labelled streams — and no widely accepted benchmark or protocol exists. The most relevant syntheses are Gomes et al. (2022, ACM CSUR survey on delayed partially labelled streams) and a dedicated 2026 experimental survey in Digital Signal Processing. The area is active but not converged: no canonical problem formulation, no dominant method.

    Well-established (safe to build on):

    • Prequential evaluation is the right protocol for ordered streams; random train/test splits are invalid (temporal leakage).
    • No drift detector dominates. Delay / false-alarm / missed-detection trade-offs depend on drift type, noise, imbalance, and configuration.
    • ARF, SRP, Leveraging Bagging, HAT are the strong adaptive baselines; Hoeffding Tree is the canonical static one.
    • Synthetic generators give ground-truth drift → detection delay is computable; real streams give external validity but unknown drift positions.
    • Elec2 has known temporal-autocorrelation problems (a "no change" baseline and Kappa+ must be reported; accuracy alone overstates learning).

    The specific holes this project targets:

    • Detector-level degradation under delay is unquantified. There is no clean curve of detection delay vs label latency, or false-alarm rate vs label coverage. Existing work studies classifiers or active-learning systems, not the detector in isolation.
    • No standard confirmation protocol combining a label-free early warning with delayed supervised confirmation. Research names this architecture explicitly as a promising, unbuilt direction.
    • The operational cost of continual learning is under-measured. Papers rarely report the latency, memory, and divergence costs of adaptation — the field is populated by ML researchers, not infrastructure people. This is our home turf.

    The bet, confirmed: the two strongest gaps (delayed labels, operational cost) both lie exactly where the field's standard assumptions diverge most sharply from production reality — and neither requires deriving novel algorithms.

    4. Research questions¶

    Three questions, one spine. Each is falsifiable and measurable.

    • RQ1 (baseline, guaranteed). Under controlled synthetic drift — sudden, gradual, incremental, recurring — how do ADWIN, DDM, EDDM, Page-Hinkley, KSWIN compare on detection delay, false-alarm rate, and missed detections, and how do adaptive models (HAT, ARF) recover compared to static ones (Hoeffding Tree, Naive Bayes)? This is the control condition: "what the literature says happens."

    • RQ2 (system, novel-by-measurement). What does running continual learning as a real service cost — p50/p99 prediction latency during adaptation, throughput ceiling, memory growth, checkpoint/restart cost, and how fast do scaled-out replicas diverge? Where does it break first?

    • RQ3 (contribution, the gap). By how much does delayed, partial, and selective labelling degrade drift detection and adaptation — exactly — and can a hybrid detector (label-free early warning + delayed supervised confirmation) recover the immediate-label baseline, or come provably close?

    The math (§6) turns RQ3 from "degrades a lot" into four specific, provable claims about how and by what factor it degrades.

    Chapter 2 — The system (infrastructure spine)¶

    A real, containerised, reproducible continual-learning service:

    • Event source: Redis Streams (zero-JVM friction) or Kafka/Redpanda — decided in Phase 2, pinned early.
    • Model service: POST /predict + a learning consumer; the same model that serves is the model that learns, one pass.
    • Live drift monitor (a real service, not an offline script).
    • Prometheus metrics + Grafana dashboard; load generator to push it until it breaks.
    • Versioned, seeded, reproducible end to end (docker-compose).

    Deliverable: the running system + the "reality tax" chapter — the operational cost the literature never reports: adaptation latency cost, replica divergence, backpressure behaviour (drop learning or drop serving?), restart/recovery cost.

    Chapter 3 — The delayed-label contribution (the research)¶

    The core novel work, built on Chapter 1's harness:

    1. Delay regimes — fixed (d = 10/100/1000), stochastic (geometric mean d), partial (label probability p ∈ {1, .5, .1}), selective (only "flagged" samples labelled — the endogenous-bias case).
    2. Measure the degradation — detector delay/FP/FN and model recovery vs. the d = 0 control, at detector level and downstream accuracy level.
    3. Build the hybrid — label-free early warning on the input stream P(X), supervised confirmation on the delayed error stream, with an explicit confirmation protocol (warning within tolerance window, confirm on labels).
    4. Evaluate — hybrid vs. naive-delayed-supervised vs. label-free-only vs. immediate-label control; test the §6 lemmas empirically.

    Fallback (safe): if the hybrid does not beat the naive baselines, that is a publishable negative result — "we quantified how much delayed labels break existing methods and why the obvious fix is insufficient." Chapter 3 cannot fail, which is why it is the bet.

    6. The mathematical angle — the delay tax¶

    The project's theory. Deliberately applied math — probability, concentration bounds, hypothesis testing — squarely within reach, and every claim is empirically verifiable in Chapter 3. This is not a bet on novel theory; it is a bet on formalising the obvious correctly and measuring the constants.

    6.1 The formal setting¶

    Let the concept be C_t ∈ {C₀, C₁}, with a real drift at time τ. The model predicts ŷ_t; the instantaneous error is

    e_t = 1[ŷ_t ≠ y_t].

    Under immediate labelling a detector observes (e_t) in real time and alarms at a stopping time T — the classical quickest detection problem (Page 1954; Lorden 1971 minimax; Shiryaev). The field's "detection delay vs false-alarm trade-off" is the empirical image of Lorden's criterion: minimise worst-case delay subject to a false-alarm constraint (average run length).

    Under label delay, each error e_t becomes observable only at t + L_t, for a latency process L_t (fixed, stochastic, or driven by a review policy). The detector's filtration is

    F_t = σ( e_s : s + L_s ≤ t ).

    This is quickest detection with delayed observations — a variant the literature has barely touched. Four claims follow, each provable with undergraduate/early-grad probability and each with a Phase-3 experiment attached.

    6.2 Lemma 1 — The reaction floor¶

    Any detector that observes only the error stream cannot alarm before the drift's labels arrive: T ≥ τ + L_τ pointwise, hence E[T] ≥ τ + E[L].

    Proof idea: e_τ ∉ F_t for t < τ + L_τ; the alarm is a stopping time w.r.t. F_t. Trivial — and that triviality is the point: it is a hard lower bound on detection delay that no algorithm can beat, independent of how good the detector is. It converts "you detect drift a month late" from a complaint into a theorem.

    Experiment: for each delay regime, measure detection delay of every detector and confirm it never dips below d (fixed case) / E[L] (stochastic case).

    6.3 Lemma 2 — The power loss (effective sample size)¶

    Under stochastic per-sample delay, a windowed detector's confidence band widens by the factor 1/√p, where p is the fraction of the window's labels that have arrived — equivalently, its minimum detectable mean shift grows from Δ_min ∝ 1/√W to Δ_min ∝ 1/√(pW).

    Proof idea: the observed error stream is a randomly thinned version of the true one; a window of W arrivals contains only ~pW distinct fresh errors, and any mean-estimate test (Hoeffding/CLT based — ADWIN's threshold is a constant times √(ln(1/δ)/W)) has standard error σ/√n with n = pW. Delay degrades sensitivity by the factor 1/√p — a clean, checkable constant.

    Experiment: for p ∈ {1, .5, .25, .1} and a fixed drift magnitude, measure ADWIN's detection delay; verify it tracks the predicted 1/√p inflation.

    6.4 Lemma 3 — False-alarm inflation under batch arrival¶

    If labels arrive in batches of size b, the observed error stream inherits intra-batch correlation ρ > 0, inflating the variance of the estimated error rate by 1 + (b−1)ρ (the standard clustered-data factor). Detectors calibrated for i.i.d. errors then fire spuriously: DDM's alarm rate rises above its nominal α.

    Proof idea: the variance of a clustered sample mean; DDM's thresholds assume e_t independent Bernoulli. Batch arrival is exactly what daily/weekly label reconciliation does in production.

    Experiment: feed detectors a delayed-error stream under batch arrival with no actual drift; measure FP rate vs. b.

    6.5 Lemma 4 — The identifiability ceiling (why the hybrid is maximal)¶

    If P(X) is unchanged but P(Y|X) differs between concepts (pure posterior drift), no label-free detector has any power: the likelihood ratio of the X-observations is identically 1, so the KL divergence between the two hypotheses is 0.

    Proof idea: the X-marginal cannot distinguish joints that share it. This is the formal content of Žliobaitė's detectability result (2010) — and it justifies the hybrid by construction: label-free signals can only be an early warning; confirmation requires labels. The hybrid is the information-maximal architecture available, which is the strongest design statement a scoping document can make.

    Experiment: construct a generator with pure posterior drift (e.g. flip P(y|x) in a region while keeping the marginal fixed); confirm label-free detectors miss it while the hybrid's delayed confirmation catches it.

    6.6 What the math is for¶

    • It gives the report a theory section with four lemmas it can actually prove, each with an empirical counterpart — rigorous, but no dependence on novel analysis (a systems-strength fit).
    • It converts Chapter 3's headline from a table of numbers into a narrative: detection is bounded below by the reaction floor, weakened by 1/√p, distorted by batch autocorrelation, and capped by identifiability — and the hybrid is the best possible response.

    6.7 Explicitly not in scope (math)¶

    • No new change-detection algorithms, no regret bounds, no online convex optimisation, no statistical learning theory, no proofs of novel theorems beyond the four lemmas above. If a lemma resists a clean proof, it degrades to an empirical claim — the project survives.

    1. Experiment harness — config-driven, seeded, reusable; one runner for Chapters 1 and 3.
    2. The running system — docker-compose stack: stream, model service, live drift monitor, Prometheus/Grafana, load generator.

    9. Scope guards¶

    ❌ Out of scope ✅ Instead
    Deep learning as the benchmark classifier Deep models are permitted only as label-free signal extractors for the hybrid detector (DF-H); the benchmark host stays classical (Hoeffding Tree / ARF / HAT)
    Regression, clustering, recsys Classification only
    Distributed training (parameter servers, federated) Single-node service; replica divergence measurement is in scope
    Building a streaming framework Use River; we build the harness and system around it
    k8s / multi-cloud production deploy docker-compose on one machine
    > 3 real datasets Exactly 3, chosen and justified
    Novel adaptation algorithms Hybrid architecture + formalised delay model + measurement
    Novel theorem-proving beyond the four lemmas Lemmas; anything harder degrades to empirical

    The single most common way student projects fail is scope creep, not difficulty.

    Scope amendment (2026-11-14): DF-H uses a deep encoder as a label-free feature monitor, not as the benchmark classifier. The classical host remains the measured learner. This implements the hybrid architecture in §6.5 and keeps the classical baseline comparison intact. If the deep monitor is disallowed, the same fusion runs on a classical PCA/KS feature monitor.

    11. Risks and fallbacks¶

    Risk Fallback
    Hybrid doesn't beat naive baselines (Ch 3) Complete, publishable negative result; lemmas 1–3 still hold and are still the contribution
    Lemma 2 or 3 proof doesn't cleanly go through Degrade to empirical claim + simulation study; report honestly
    Phase 2 (system) overruns Shrink Phase 3; Chapter 2 is still a complete chapter
    Elec2 results are contested Pre-empt with no-change baseline + Kappa+ + exact preprocessing statement
    Scope creep (the real killer) §9 guards; anything new must pass the "which research question does this serve?" test

    Every chapter has a survivable failure mode; no single bet can sink the project.

    12. Open decisions (teacher-dependent — must be asked)¶

    • Does the teacher require novelty, or is rigorous measurement/comparison acceptable? (This decides how hard Chapter 3 has to land.)
    • Report only, or report + working system? (This scope assumes both; a report-only variant drops Phase 2/3 to optional.)
    • Required format/length (IEEE two-column?), interim milestones, individual or group, deadline.

    These change packaging, not substance. The spine survives any answer.
